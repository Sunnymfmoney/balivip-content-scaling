# The sources, compressed to what changes a draft

## Breakthrough Advertising, Eugene Schwartz

**Desire cannot be created, only channelled.** Mass desire already exists in the market. The
headline's job is to take that desire and attach it to your product.

**Five stages of awareness.** Most Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware,
Most Aware. The headline changes completely at each stage. Writing product-aware copy to a
problem-aware reader is the single most common failure.

**Three stages of market sophistication.** First to make a claim, you state it plainly.
Second, you make it bigger. Third, you need a new mechanism. Fourth, you elaborate the
mechanism. Fifth, you leave the claim and identify with the reader instead. Know which stage
your market is in before you write a headline, or you will make a claim they have heard
three times.

**Gradualisation.** Do not ask a cold reader to accept the big claim. Walk them through
statements they already agree with until the claim is the only place left to land.

## StoryBrand, Donald Miller

**The customer is the hero, never the brand.** The brand is the guide.

Seven beats: a character wants something, hits a problem, meets a guide with empathy and
authority, who gives them a plan, calls them to action, and shows what success and failure
look like.

**Empathy plus authority.** "We know how it feels" alone is soft. Authority alone is cold.
Both, in that order.

**A plan removes fear.** Three or four named steps beat any adjective. People do not buy
what they cannot picture doing.

**Grunt test.** In five seconds, can a stranger say what you offer, how it makes their life
better, and what to do next? If not, nothing else matters.

## $100M Offers and $100M Leads, Alex Hormozi

**Value equation.** (Dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (time delay ×
effort and sacrifice). Four levers, and most writers only pull one. Cutting time and effort
usually beats adding benefits.

**Sell the vehicle, not the ingredients.** Nobody wants a coaching call. They want the
result the call produces.

**Name the deliverable, the timeframe and what it costs them to try.** Vagueness reads as
risk.

**Make the ask so specific it is easy to say yes to.** "Message us" is not an ask. "Comment
VIP and we send X today" is.

## Expert Secrets and DotCom Secrets, Russell Brunson

**Hook, story, offer.** Every unit, at every length. Miss the hook and the rest is unread.

**The one thing.** A piece carries one idea. A carousel with three arguments has none.

**The Epiphany Bridge.** Tell the moment you realised the old way was broken. People adopt
new beliefs through a story about a change of mind, not through a list of features.

**Break the false belief before you sell.** Name the thing they wrongly believe, show why it
fails, then give the new mechanism. This is why "how not to" and "the mistake everyone
makes" formats convert: they break a belief before asking for anything.

## Where they agree, which is what to trust

- Lead with the reader's existing desire, never with your product.
- Break a false belief before you make a claim.
- A named plan beats an adjective.
- One idea, one ask.
- Specificity is the cheapest credibility there is.
- Cutting effort and delay is worth more than adding benefits.
